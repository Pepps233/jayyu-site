One of the smallest and most accessible multimodal models is **Qwen3.5-0.8B**, and this experiment pairs it with the next two sizes up in the same family: **Qwen3.5-2B** and **Qwen3.5-4B**.
The focus is to measure how much a small model gains from distillation with a larger teacher, and whether it can outscore a model 2.5x its size.

On a frozen sample of **500 BFCL-V4 single-turn tasks**, the stock 0.8B model averaged **57.80%** accuracy.
Distilled with Qwen3.5-2B as the teacher, which provides another comparison baseline, the 0.8B model reached **60.80%**, but its accuracy on no-call tasks fell from **79.11% to 38.22%**.
Distilled with the Qwen3.5-4B teacher instead, it reached **71.07% overall** and **81.11% on no-call tasks**.

The [final distilled 0.8B model](https://huggingface.co/Pepps233/Qwen3.5-0.8B-ToolCalling-OPD)[^opd-model] scores about **6 percentage points higher** than stock Qwen3.5-2B, a model 2.5x its size, on these single-turn tool calls.

For comparison, the same 0.8B model was also distilled from the same 4B teacher through [full supervised fine-tuning](https://huggingface.co/Pepps233/Qwen3.5-0.8B-ToolCalling-SFT)[^sft-model], which reached **71.67%**, about the same score as on-policy distillation.

## What the student should learn

Tool calling asks a model to turn a request into a structured function call: choose the right function and supply the right arguments.
A single request can require several calls, and sometimes none of the available functions can help.
In that last case, declining to call a tool is the correct answer. These are **no-call tasks**.

For example, imagine asking for a restaurant reservation when the only available tool checks the weather forecast.
A well-formed weather call would still be wrong, because it doesn't solve the problem at hand.

## Training setup

**SkyRL** was used for on-policy distillation ([Agarwal et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2306.13649))[^agarwal] on two H100s.
The student generated fresh responses to the selected prompts, and the teacher scored the exact tokens the student had produced.
Those teacher scores supplied the learning signal for each student weight update, and after each update, the rollout engine received the new student weights.

For each sampled token, the reward is:

<p class="formula">r<sub>t</sub> = log p<sub>teacher</sub>(y<sub>t</sub> | prefix) − log p<sub>student</sub>(y<sub>t</sub> | prefix)</p>

Here, y<sub>t</sub> is the student's t-th token, and the prefix is the prompt plus every token sampled before it.
Summed over a response, these rewards form a single-sample estimate of the negative reverse KL divergence from the student to the teacher.

<QwenDistillationDiagram kind="training" />

The frozen teacher ran on one H100, while the student learner and rollout engine shared the other, using Modal for compute and vLLM for serving.

Settings:

- **Thinking disabled:** short, structured answers mean less generation and scoring overhead.
- **A 1,024-token output cap and 16,384-token context:** single-turn tool calls are short, so 1,024 output tokens is generally enough.
- **Four prompts per batch, one rollout per prompt:** this keeps the training loop small.
- **One epoch at a learning rate of `1e-6`:** a starting estimate rather than a tuned value.
- **A checkpoint and Hugging Face export every five updates:** intermediate versions stay available for inspection and recovery.

Before the full run, check tokenizer compatibility so that the teacher and student scores refer to the same sampled token IDs.

## Selecting training data

The training data came from a pool of 6,500 examples derived from [xLAM](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Salesforce/xlam-function-calling-60k) and [ToolACE](https://huggingface.co/datasets/Team-ACE/ToolACE).
These examples were converted locally into single-turn requests with tool definitions and reference calls, and 325 were set aside for held-out evaluation.

Of the 6,500 examples, 5,000 were screened as candidate prompts by running the stock 0.8B student twice on each one.
If either attempt failed, the prompt went to the teacher, including prompts where the student succeeded once and failed once.
In total, the student failed at least one attempt on 2,911 prompts.

The 2B teacher passed 1,030 of those 2,911 prompts.
Keeping the ones it also passed in at least two of three fresh consistency attempts left 877 prompts for the first distilled model.
The 4B teacher passed 1,293 of the same 2,911 prompts (44.42%), and the same rule left 1,185.

## The 500 BFCL-V4 tasks

A uniform random sample of 500 examples was frozen, without replacement, from the 3,641 examples in the single-turn portion of the [Berkeley Function Calling Leaderboard (BFCL)](https://gorilla.cs.berkeley.edu/leaderboard.html).
BFCL's [pinned category definitions](https://github.com/ShishirPatil/gorilla/blob/6ea57973c7a6097fd7c5915698c54c17c5b1b6c8/berkeley-function-call-leaderboard/bfcl_eval/constants/category_mapping.py) separate these single-turn tasks from the benchmark's multi-turn, memory, and web-search categories.

The sample contains 150 no-call tasks and 350 other call/relevance tasks.
It covers simple calls, choosing among multiple functions, parallel calls, and irrelevance detection, including Python, Java, and JavaScript cases.

For scoring, the pinned official BFCL preprocessing, function-call decoder, and AST/relevance checkers were used through a local adapter.
Each model made three attempts on every task with identical settings: thinking disabled, temperature `0.6`, top-p `0.95`, top-k `20`, a 1,024-token output cap, and BF16 serving.
The mean below averages the three run accuracies.
Note that 1,500 responses per model still represent only 500 distinct problems.

## A stronger teacher and more no-call coverage

After distilling the stock 0.8B model with the 2B teacher, the 4B teacher was used for a separate run, again starting from stock 0.8B.
Only 86 of the first run's 877 prompts (9.8%) were no-call examples, while the BFCL sample is 30% no-call.

After applying the same two-of-three rule, 1,185 prompts were left, including only 133 no-call examples.
The 4B teacher also attempted 350 additional no-call candidates three times each, and the 292 it passed at least twice were added to the training set.
Unlike the screened prompts, these were not required to be student failures.
The goal was to prevent the same drop in no-call accuracy and to keep the training set from being dominated by call tasks.

The resulting dataset contained 1,477 prompts: 541 xLAM calls, 511 ToolACE calls, and 425 no-call examples (~29%).

On the unchanged BFCL sample, the new distilled model reached **71.07%** overall, a gain of **13.27 percentage points** over stock 0.8B and **10.27 points** over the 0.8B model distilled from 2B.
No-call accuracy recovered to **81.11%**, and the specialized student also exceeded stock 2B's **65.13%** on this sample.

There was still a slight tradeoff.
The new model passed 701 of 1,050 attempts on the 350 call/relevance tasks, or 66.76%, compared with 70.48% for the model distilled from 2B.
The large recovery in no-call behavior came with a smaller decline on the other tasks.

## Comparison with supervised fine-tuning

The 4B teacher was also distilled into stock 0.8B through supervised fine-tuning (SFT).
Instead of scoring fresh student rollouts, SFT trains on fixed targets: one verified, non-truncated 4B answer for each of the same 1,477 prompts.
It updated every weight of the text model, without LoRA, for three epochs at batch size 16 and a learning rate of `2e-5` on a single H100.
Because that differs from the on-policy run above in epochs, batch size, and update count, a second on-policy run matched SFT's batch size of 16 and three epochs while keeping the learning rate at `1e-6`.

<QwenDistillationDiagram kind="sft" />

SFT (71.67%) and the matched on-policy run (71.40%) differ by four correct responses out of 1,500, which doesn't establish a winner.
The split differs more: SFT was strongest on no-call tasks at 87.56%, while the matched on-policy run was strongest on the other 350 tasks at 72.00% but fell to 70.00% on no-call.
One plausible but unverified explanation is that SFT trains directly on correct teacher answers, including correct abstentions, while the teacher's token preferences on the student's own attempts can still favor a plausible call.
Learning rate, optimizer settings, and loss also differ, so this comparison does not isolate the two methods.

## For the future

Keep the teacher and training-set size fixed while varying the no-call proportion.
This would help separate the effect of coverage from the effect of a stronger teacher or simply more training prompts.
